Autonomy is a platform setting

WILLIAM · starts 33:45 · 45 s

WILLIAM

So, autonomy, and the human in the loop.

[sweep left to right along the bar]

Read it left to right, by amount. Under the line, the agent pays on its own. Above it, a person decides. Past the fifty-thousand ceiling, nobody: the gateway refuses, whoever asks.

Where that line sits is one value in the platform: the autonomy flag. Assisted puts it at zero, so every payout goes to a person. Autonomous puts it at six thousand. Nothing is redeployed to move it, and it moves back just as fast. And the agent can't touch it. That's the keystone.

You saw it in the demo: fourteen fifty paid alone, eighty-five hundred waited for a person, and eighty thousand, sent in the agent's own name, refused outright.

"The human is not removed. The platform decides where the human stands."

And the evidence, run after run, graded from the trace, is what earns the next notch.

What the slide is for

It reframes "human in the loop" from a yes/no property of an agent into a position set by the platform. It ties together three mechanisms the audience saw separately: the policy service's limit (the line), the gateway's CEL ceiling (the hatched zone) and the OpenFeature flag (what moves the line). The last sentence links Prove back to Decide: evaluation results are the evidence a team uses to justify moving the line.

The theory behind it

Plain words. Think of a new employee's signing authority. Day one: everything needs a manager's signature. After a few months of clean work: up to a limit on their own. Some things nobody signs alone, ever. The employee doesn't set their own limit; the organisation does, on the record. Here the "organisation" is the platform, and the record is a reviewed commit.

Why the ceiling is separate. CEL holds the fact that never changes and needs no lookup; the line depends on who asks and today's level, so it needs the policy service. Two independent layers: a bug in one doesn't remove the other.

Configuration and options

# pds/rules.py
EXECUTE_LIMITS = {
  (".../sa/settlement-agent", "assisted"):   0.0,
  (".../sa/settlement-agent", "autonomous"): 6000.0,
}
# featureflag.yaml: committed default for the settlement agent is "assisted";
# scripts/demo start / scripts/demo autonomy autonomous flips it live.
  • Moving the line in practice: change the flag variant (a one-word diff on the FeatureFlag), reviewed and applied by a person or pipeline. Kyverno denies the change from any agent identity.
  • Fail-safe direction: if flagd can't answer, the level falls back to assisted (limit €0). Unknown caller or unknown level means deny.
  • Measuring whether to move it: the approval-wait histogram by autonomy level, approval vs reject counts, and AgentEvals results across runs.
  • Finer dials (not built): per-claim-type or per-customer-segment limits via flag targeting on more context, or more levels in the table.

Alternatives

  • Limit in the prompt. Probabilistic; the opening slides showed why it can't hold.
  • Limit as an env var or config in the agent. Deterministic, but needs a redeploy and lives where the agent's own deployment pipeline can change it.
  • Limit in the gateway's CEL only. Works for a fixed number; can't do per-level lookups or ledger state, and a CEL deny names no rule in a decision span.

If someone asks

Who decides to move the line?
Whoever owns the risk, through a reviewed change. The platform makes it a one-word diff and records every decision made under each level.
Can the line move per customer?
The flag targets by SPIFFE ID today. OpenFeature targeting can use any evaluation context, so yes in principle; the table would gain rows.
